# Budgeting and Saving

Practical, educational concepts for managing day-to-day money. General information, not
personalised advice.

## What a budget is

A budget is a plan that matches expected **income** against expected **expenses** over a
period (usually a month), so money is directed on purpose rather than by accident. It is not
about restriction for its own sake; it is a tool to make sure spending reflects priorities
and that saving actually happens.

## Building a budget: a step-by-step method

1. **Add up net (take-home) income** for the month — salary after tax and deductions, plus
   any reliable side income.
2. **List fixed expenses** — rent, EMIs, insurance premiums, school fees, subscriptions.
3. **Estimate variable expenses** — groceries, transport, utilities, eating out,
   entertainment. Use the last 2–3 months of bank and card statements to get real numbers.
4. **Set savings and debt-repayment targets** — treat these as bills to "pay yourself"
   first, ideally by automatic transfer on payday.
5. **Compare totals.** If expenses + savings exceed income, adjust the flexible categories
   or increase income. If there's a surplus, assign it a job (extra savings, debt, a
   specific goal).
6. **Track through the month** and review at month-end. Adjust the plan; budgeting is
   iterative.

## Popular budgeting frameworks

- **50 / 30 / 20** — roughly 50% of net income to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings and
  debt repayment. A starting guideline, not a rule; high living costs may push the "needs"
  share up.
- **Zero-based budgeting** — every unit of income is assigned to a category until income
  minus allocations equals zero. Precise but more effort.
- **Envelope / jar method** — cash or separate digital wallets per category; when an
  envelope is empty, spending in that category stops for the month.
- **Pay-yourself-first** — automate savings and investments immediately on payday, then
  spend what remains.

## Emergency fund

- A reserve of easily accessible money for genuine emergencies: job loss, medical costs,
  urgent repairs.
- Common guidance is **3–6 months of essential expenses**; those with irregular income or
  dependants may aim higher.
- Keep it **liquid and low-risk** — a separate savings account or a liquid fund — not in
  volatile investments and not mixed with the spending account.
- Rebuild it first after any use.

## Sinking funds (planned irregular costs)

Set aside a little each month for predictable but non-monthly expenses — insurance renewals,
festivals, annual maintenance, travel — so they don't become "emergencies" or go on a card.

## Managing irregular or low income

- Budget on a **lowest-expected-month** basis; treat surplus in good months as buffer.
- Prioritise in order: essential food and housing, minimum debt payments, then everything
  else.
- Look into government schemes and subsidies you may be entitled to.

## Saving more: common levers

- **Automate** the transfer so saving isn't a monthly decision.
- **Review recurring charges** — subscriptions, unused memberships, higher-tier plans.
- **Big three costs** — housing, transport, food usually dominate budgets; small percentage
  cuts here beat cutting tiny discretionary items.
- **Avoid lifestyle creep** — when income rises, direct part of the raise to savings before
  adjusting spending.
- **Use windfalls deliberately** — bonuses, tax refunds, gifts: decide a split (e.g. part to
  goals, part to enjoy) in advance.
- **Delay non-essential purchases** — a waiting period (e.g. 30 days) reduces impulse
  buying.

## Setting financial goals

Make goals **specific, measurable, and time-bound**: "₹3,00,000 for a car down payment in
30 months" implies saving ₹10,000/month. Separate goals by horizon:

- **Short term (<1 yr):** keep in savings / liquid form.
- **Medium term (1–5 yrs):** lower-volatility options.
- **Long term (>5 yrs):** can tolerate more volatility for growth.

## Tracking tools

A notebook, a spreadsheet, or an app all work. What matters is consistency and reviewing the
numbers. Categorise transactions, compare to plan monthly, and look at 3–6 month trends
rather than obsessing over a single day.

## Dealing with a deficit or debt spiral

- List every debt with its balance, rate, and minimum payment.
- Keep paying **all** minimums to protect your credit record.
- Direct extra money to either the **highest-rate** debt (avalanche — cheapest overall) or
  the **smallest balance** (snowball — faster wins for motivation).
- Contact lenders early about hardship options; don't borrow new high-cost debt to pay old
  debt.
- Be cautious with "debt settlement" offers — they can damage your credit and may have
  hidden costs.
