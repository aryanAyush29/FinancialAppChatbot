# Investing Basics

Educational introduction to investment concepts and common instruments. This is general
information, **not** investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

## Saving vs. investing

- **Saving** puts money aside in low-risk, liquid places (savings account, FD). The nominal
  amount is safe, but inflation slowly erodes its purchasing power.
- **Investing** puts money into assets that can grow in value or pay income, accepting the
  risk of short-term loss in exchange for the potential of higher long-term returns.

## The risk–return relationship

Higher expected return generally comes with higher risk (bigger and more frequent ups and
downs, and a real chance of loss). No legitimate investment offers high returns with no
risk. Promises of "guaranteed" high returns are a classic sign of a scam.

Types of risk include market risk (prices fall), credit/default risk (a borrower doesn't
repay), inflation risk (returns don't keep up with prices), liquidity risk (can't sell
quickly at a fair price), concentration risk (too much in one place), and currency risk.

## Key principles

- **Diversification** — spreading money across asset types, sectors, and geographies so one
  bad outcome doesn't dominate.
- **Time horizon** — how long until you need the money. Longer horizons can ride out
  volatility; money needed soon generally belongs in low-risk forms.
- **Compounding** — reinvested returns earn their own returns. Small differences in rate or
  time compound into large differences in outcome.
- **Rupee-cost / dollar-cost averaging** — investing a fixed amount at regular intervals
  (e.g. a monthly SIP) buys more units when prices are low and fewer when high, smoothing
  the entry price.
- **Costs matter** — fees, expense ratios, commissions, and taxes reduce returns every year.
- **Asset allocation** — the split between broad categories (equity, debt, cash, gold, real
  estate) usually drives results more than individual security picks.

## Common instruments

### Equity / stocks / shares
Part-ownership of a company. Returns come from price appreciation and dividends. Historically
higher long-run returns than most other asset classes, but with large drawdowns. Buying
individual stocks concentrates risk and needs research; many people get equity exposure
through funds instead.

### Bonds / fixed income
A loan to a government or company that pays periodic interest (coupon) and returns the face
value at maturity. Generally less volatile than equity. Prices fall when interest rates
rise. Government bonds carry low default risk; corporate bonds pay more to compensate for
higher risk. In India, examples include G-Secs, T-bills, and instruments like the RBI
Floating Rate Savings Bond.

### Mutual funds
A pooled vehicle managed by a fund house that invests many investors' money per a stated
objective. Types include equity, debt, hybrid, index, and liquid funds. Key features: NAV
(net asset value per unit), **expense ratio** (annual cost), open-ended vs. close-ended, and
regular vs. **direct** plans (direct plans have lower cost because they exclude distributor
commission). SIPs automate regular investing.

### Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) / index funds
Funds that track an index (e.g. Nifty 50, S&P 500) rather than trying to beat it. Low cost
and broad diversification. ETFs trade on an exchange like a stock; index mutual funds are
bought from the fund house at end-of-day NAV.

### Government small-savings and retirement schemes (India)
PPF, EPF, NPS, Sukanya Samriddhi, Senior Citizens' Savings Scheme, and NSC — government-
backed, with defined rules on lock-in, contribution limits, and tax treatment.

### Real estate
Property for rental income and/or appreciation. High transaction costs, low liquidity, and
concentration risk; REITs offer a more liquid, smaller-ticket way to get some exposure.

### Gold
Held as physical metal, Sovereign Gold Bonds, or gold ETFs. Often used as a diversifier and
inflation hedge; it pays no income (except SGB interest) and can be volatile.

### Cash and cash equivalents
Savings accounts, liquid funds, money-market instruments. Low return, high liquidity — the
place for an emergency fund and near-term needs.

## Things to be cautious about

- **Ponzi / pyramid schemes** — returns paid from new investors' money, not real profits.
  Warning signs: guaranteed high returns, pressure to recruit others, vague strategy,
  difficulty withdrawing.
- **"Hot tips", pump-and-dump, and finfluencer stock calls** — often manipulation or paid
  promotion.
- **Unregistered products and advisers** — in India, check SEBI registration for advisers,
  research analysts, and intermediaries; verify entities on the regulator's site.
- **Leverage / F&O trading** — derivatives can multiply losses; studies by SEBI show most
  individual intraday and F&O traders lose money.
- **Crypto assets** — highly volatile, largely unregulated for investor protection, and
  taxed at a flat rate in India with TDS on transfers. Only ever risk money you can afford
  to lose.

## A simple mental framework (educational, not advice)

1. First build an **emergency fund** (a few months of expenses) in safe, liquid form.
2. Clear **high-interest debt** (like revolving credit-card balances) — that's a guaranteed
   "return" equal to the interest rate.
3. Use appropriate **insurance** (health, term life) so a shock doesn't force asset sales.
4. Then invest for goals, matching risk to **time horizon**, keeping costs low, staying
   **diversified**, and reviewing periodically rather than reacting to every headline.
5. For anything complex or large, consider a **registered, fee-only financial adviser**.
